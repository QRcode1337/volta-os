- **Canvas:** faceless, flat stick-figure animation on a dark background (the @brad_learns_ai pattern). Scammer in red, victim in white, the fix in EPSILON green. One big caption line on screen at all times.
- **Structure:** hook (0–3 s) → how the scam plays out (dramatized) → the one move that beats it → sign-off.
- **Sign-off (same every time):** "One scam a day. Follow so you see the next one before they call you." Pinned comment: epsilonsec.ai.
- **Voice:** your own voice builds the most trust. If you use an AI voice, turn on TikTok's AI-generated content label. It's required, and outliers show the label doesn't kill reach.

## 1. The AI voice-clone "Mom, I'm in trouble" call

**Hook (on screen):** "Your kid's voice. Crying. It's not your kid."

**VO:** Your phone rings. It's your son, crying. He's been in an accident and needs money right now. It sounds exactly like him, because a scammer cloned his voice from a 10-second video online. Here's the fix. Tonight, pick a family safe word. Something nobody could guess, never posted anywhere. Anyone calling in an emergency has to say it. No word? Hang up and call them back on the number already in your phone. Caller ID can be faked. Your safe word can't.

**Visual:** phone buzzing → waveform copied from a social video → parent reaching for wallet → freeze, green "SAFE WORD?" stamp.
**Caption:** Set this up tonight. Send it to your family group chat. #scamalert #aiscam #voiceclone #safeword
**Verify:** the FTC family-emergency guidance (safe word, call back on a known number). Soften "10 seconds" to "a short clip" if you can't source it.

## 2. Why scammers wait before speaking

**Hook:** "You answer. Silence. Hang up. Here's why."

**VO:** Ever pick up and nobody's there for a few seconds? That's often an auto-dialer. It calls thousands of numbers and only connects a human scammer when it detects a live voice. Your "hello?" tells it this number is real and someone answers. That number goes on a list and gets more calls. The move: if you answer an unknown number and hear silence, don't talk. Just hang up. Better, let unknown numbers go to voicemail. Real people leave messages.

**Visual:** a wall of phones ringing → one "HELLO?" lights up green on a scammer dashboard → number added to a list.
**Verify:** auto-dialer behavior is well documented. Don't claim that the pause is used to record your voice for cloning.

## 3. The empty envelope: what it means, and what's a rumor

**Hook:** "Got an empty envelope in the mail?"

**VO:** An envelope shows up with your name on it and nothing inside. The internet says burglars are checking whether you're home. There's no official warning that proves that. Here's what it more often means: someone is confirming your name and address are real. That's usually tied to a "brushing" scheme or to your data being sold. What to do: check your bank and credit for anything new, freeze your credit (it's free), and report it at usps.com/postalinspectors. Don't panic. Lock it down.

**Visual:** envelope opens to nothing → rumor bubble crossed out → green checklist.
**Verify:** I couldn't confirm a Postal Inspection Service warning about blank envelopes, so the script says that on purpose. Keep the debunk framing.

## 4. "Quishing": one QR scan

**Hook:** "Don't scan that parking meter QR code."

**VO:** Scammers are sticking fake QR codes over real ones on parking meters, on flyers, even in emails. You scan it, you get a payment page that looks official, and you just handed over your card. It's called quishing. The fix: before you tap the link, look at the web address your camera shows you. If it's not the real company's site, stop. For parking, use the official app or pay at the machine. And if a sticker is covering a code, that's your answer.

**Visual:** sticker peeled off a meter revealing the real code underneath.
**Verify:** the FTC has warned about QR-code scams. Cite it if you add a source on screen.

## 5. The fake "$900 charge" text, then the "bank" calls

**Hook:** "Did you make a $900 purchase? Reply YES or NO."

**VO:** You get a text about a charge you didn't make. You reply NO. Minutes later, the "fraud department" calls, and they already know your name and bank. They say your account is compromised and you need to move your money to a safe account, or read them a code we just sent. Both are the scam. Your bank will never ask you to move money to protect it, and never ask you to read a code back. Hang up. Call the number on the back of your card.

**Visual:** text bubble → phone rings → "SAFE ACCOUNT" turns red → card flipped to show the phone number.
**Verify:** the FTC guidance that banks don't ask you to move money or read back a code.

## 6. The Zelle "send it back to yourself" scam

**Hook:** "Your bank will never ask you to Zelle yourself."

**VO:** The "bank" calls about suspicious activity. To reverse it, they say, send the money to yourself through Zelle. They even put your name on it. But the account behind it is theirs. Zelle moves money like cash, and once it's sent it's very hard to get back. Rule: nobody legitimate asks you to send money to fix fraud. Hang up and call your bank yourself.

**Visual:** "To: YOU" label peels off to reveal "SCAMMER".
**Verify:** Zelle's and the bank-industry warnings about this exact script.

## 7. Are your calls secretly being forwarded?

**Hook:** "Dial this to see if someone's getting your calls."

**VO:** There's a scam where someone gets you to dial a short code, often pretending to be your carrier. That code forwards your calls, including your bank's verification calls, to them. Check it now. On most phones, dial star-pound-two-one-pound (*#21#). It shows whether forwarding is on. To cancel all forwarding, dial ##002#. On Verizon it's *73. And never dial a code a caller tells you to. Your carrier won't ask.

**On-screen codes:** `*#21#` check · `##002#` cancel (AT&T / T-Mobile) · `*73` cancel (Verizon)
**Verify:** carrier codes vary. Test each code on an AT&T, a T-Mobile and a Verizon line before posting, or add "codes vary by carrier."

## 8. The settings that stop scammers seeing your number

**Hook:** "Three phone settings scammers hate."

**VO:** One: on iPhone, turn on Silence Unknown Callers. On Android, turn on spam call filtering. Real callers leave voicemail. Two: hide your caller ID when you call businesses you don't trust. It's in your phone's call settings, or dial *67 before a single call. Three: ask your carrier to add a port-out PIN, so nobody can move your number to a new SIM. That one stops account takeovers.

**Visual:** three toggles flipping green.
**Verify:** the menu paths for the current iOS and Android versions, and each carrier's name for the port-out PIN.

## 9. Amazon scams going around right now

**Hook:** "Amazon didn't text you."

**VO:** Three Amazon scams to know. One: "Your order of an iPhone is confirmed. Call to cancel." The number goes to a scammer. Two: "Your Prime account is suspended. Log in here." That's a fake login page. Three: a refund that "overpaid" you, and now they need you to send the difference back. Fix all three the same way: open the Amazon app yourself and check your orders. If it's not there, it's not real.

**Verify:** Amazon's own scam page lists these patterns.

## 10. Deepfake celebrity ads

**Hook:** "That celebrity never recommended this."

**VO:** You're scrolling and a famous face is pitching a crypto platform, a miracle supplement, or a giveaway. The voice is right. The lips mostly match. It's a deepfake ad. Scammers rent real-looking faces with AI and buy ads on the same platforms you trust. The tell isn't the video, it's the offer: guaranteed returns, a deadline, "limited spots." Search the celebrity's name plus "scam." If it's real, it'll be on their own verified account.

**Verify:** keep it generic. Don't name a specific celebrity unless a news source confirms that deepfake.

## 11. Spotting an AI-faked video

**Hook:** "Is this video real? Stop checking the hands."

**VO:** The old tells, like weird hands and blinking, are disappearing as AI gets better. So check the source, not the pixels. One: who posted it first? A brand-new account is a red flag. Two: is any real news outlet reporting it? Three: does it want you to act fast, share, buy or donate? Four: reverse-search a screenshot. The fakes are getting perfect. The stories behind them aren't.

**Visual:** magnifying glass over pixels gets crossed out → swaps to a magnifying glass over the account profile.

## 12. "Fake cops" demanding payment

**Hook:** "The police will never call you for Bitcoin."

**VO:** The caller says he's a deputy. You missed jury duty, or there's a warrant, and you can pay the fine right now: gift cards, Bitcoin ATM, wire. Real police don't call to collect payment, and no government office takes gift cards or crypto. Hang up, look up your local department's real number, and call it yourself.

**Verify:** the FTC/FBI government-impersonation guidance.

## 13. AI agents and your bank login (timely)

**Hook:** "Don't give your bank password to your AI assistant."

**VO:** AI assistants can now shop, pay bills and log into sites for you. And banks are pushing back. Bank of America emailed customers this month asking them not to share their banking login with outside services, including AI agents, and warned that customers can be held responsible for what happens with that access. Other big banks are blocking agents outright. The rule: never hand any app or AI your bank password. Use your bank's own app or its official connections.

**Verify:** this is current news (late Oct 2026). Re-check before posting, and cite it on screen ("Bank of America email, Oct 2026"). Sources: [Android Authority](https://www.androidauthority.com/bank-of-america-ai-agent-password-warning-3728947/), [Moneylife](https://www.moneylife.in/article/us-banks-block-outside-ai-agents-over-fears-they-could-access-accounts-and-move-money-without-clear-authorisation-reports/82014.html).

## 14. The fake bank login page

**Hook:** "Googling your bank's login? Stop."

**VO:** Scammers buy search ads that look exactly like your bank and sit above the real result. You click, you log in, and they have your password and your code. The fix takes 10 seconds: use your bank's app, or type the address yourself once and bookmark it. Never log in from a search result, a text or an email link. Send this to anyone who Googles their bank.

**Visual:** search results with the fake ad on top, bookmark star turning green.

## 15. The AI romance scam

**Hook:** "They're perfect, and they've never been on video with you."

**VO:** You meet someone online. Attentive, gorgeous, always available. Now AI writes their messages and generates their photos. But the pattern hasn't changed. They can't video call, or the call glitches. There's an emergency, or an "investment" they want you in on. Then they ask for money, gift cards or crypto. The rule: never send money to someone you haven't met in person. Not once, not for any reason.

**Verify:** the FTC romance-scam guidance. No loss figures unless sourced.

## 16–20 (not outlier-checked)

## 16. The unpaid toll text

**Hook:** "You owe $4.99 in tolls? No, you don't."

**VO:** A text says you have an unpaid toll and a late fee is coming. Pay here. It's a fake site built to take your card. Toll agencies have warned about this for years, and the FBI has too. Don't tap. Go to your toll account yourself, or call the number on your toll statement.

**Verify:** the FBI IC3 toll-smishing alert.

## 17. The "wrong number" text

**Hook:** "Sorry, wrong number 😊. It's not."

**VO:** "Hi, is this Mike? Oh sorry, wrong number." You're polite, they're friendly, and weeks later your new friend has a crypto app that's making them rich. This is how pig-butchering scams start: slow, friendly, patient. Then the "investment" balance grows, and you can never withdraw. If a stranger from a wrong-number text ever mentions investing, block them.

## 18. The tech-support pop-up

**Hook:** "Your computer is NOT infected. That pop-up is."

**VO:** Your screen locks, an alarm blares, and a message says call Microsoft or Apple now. Real companies don't put phone numbers in pop-up warnings. Don't call, and never let anyone remote into your computer. Close the browser. If it won't close, restart the computer. That's it.

**Verify:** Microsoft's and Apple's statements that they don't put phone numbers in error messages.

## 19. Gift cards are the tell

**Hook:** "If they want gift cards, it's a scam. Every time."

**VO:** The IRS, the police, your utility company, your boss, tech support. None of them take payment in gift cards. The moment anyone asks you to buy gift cards and read them the numbers, you know. And cashiers: if an older customer is buying a stack of gift cards and looks stressed, ask them who it's for.

**Verify:** FTC gift-card scam guidance.

## 20. SIM swap: how they steal your number

**Hook:** "They didn't hack your phone. They stole your number."

**VO:** A scammer calls your carrier pretending to be you and moves your number to their SIM. Now your texts and codes go to them, and they reset your bank and email passwords. Your phone just says "No service." Two fixes: add a port-out or account PIN with your carrier, and switch your important accounts from text codes to an authenticator app.

**Verify:** each carrier's name for the PIN (Verizon "Number Lock", T-Mobile "SIM protection", AT&T "Wireless Account Lock"). Confirm the current names before posting.
